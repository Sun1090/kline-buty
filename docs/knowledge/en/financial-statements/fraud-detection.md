# Detecting Financial Fraud

> **<mark>Financial fraud</mark>** is the most expensive tuition in A-share history. This article maps fraud motives and techniques in full, reviews the convicted 2020 Luckin Coffee case, lays out the "three highs" signals, audit-opinion types, and a detection toolbox, and shows you how to use CSRC and exchange inquiry letters as free due diligence. **Content draws only on publicly disclosed information and convicted cases; no non-convicted company is named.**

---

## 1. Fraud Motives: Why Anyone Takes This Risk

| Motive | Scenario |
|---|---|
| Financing | Inflated profits support high-priced placements/rights issues/bond issuance |
| Propping up the stock price | Major shareholders need price cooperation for sell-downs, pledges, option exercises |
| Earnings commitments | During M&A earn-out periods, targets get "manufactured" when they fall short |
| Avoiding delisting | Consecutive losses risk **<mark>delisting</mark>** or ST designation — profit becomes life support |
| Manipulating share prices | Coordinating with operators to pump and dump; profit is just a prop |
| Management self-interest | Bonuses tied to results, stock options |

> The pattern: **fraud almost always has a "need" — needing financing, market cap, or to hit targets.** First ask "who most needs this profit", then judge its quality.

::: danger 💀 Iron Law: Ask "Who Most Needs This Profit", Then Judge Its Quality
**Fraud almost always has a "need" — financing, market cap, hitting targets.** So the first move in reading a report is not computing ratios but asking: is the major shareholder short of cash? In an earn-out period? Fighting to avoid delisting? Finding the "need" first, then judging profit quality, screens out most mines.
:::

## 2. The Full Map of Fraud Techniques

### Technique 1: Inflating Revenue (Most Common, Most Prevalent)

| Sub-technique | Operation | Traces |
|---|---|---|
| Fabricated customers | Setting up shell companies as buyers, issuing IOU-style sales | Receivables exploding abnormally; customers highly concentrated with no verifiable substance |
| Round-trip transactions | Two related parties selling the same goods back and forth to create revenue flow | Revenue and purchases inflated in lockstep; abnormal gross margin; cash flow inconsistent with revenue scale |
| Premature recognition | Recognizing revenue before delivery, payment, or completion of service | Contracts mismatched with revenue; revenue bunched at quarter-end |
| Channel stuffing | Overshipping distributors with return promises, forcing goods "into the channel" | Distorted channel inventory; end-market sales diverging from reported figures |

### Technique 2: Understating Costs

- Under-transferring costs: leaving too much in inventory, booking too little cost → inflated gross profit.
- Under-provisioning depreciation/amortization: stretching useful lives, lowering salvage values.
- Skipping impairments: bad debts, inventory write-downs, asset impairments that "should be booked but aren't".

### Technique 3: Hiding Liabilities

| Method | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Off-balance-sheet debt | Guarantees, repurchase obligations, disguised debt-as-equity kept off the books |
| Related-party fund diversion | Money lent to related parties, showing up as receivables or prepayments |
| Concealed litigation/guarantees | Contingent liabilities undisclosed; risk accumulates "off the books" |

### Technique 4: Inflating Assets

| Item | Method | Why Hard to Detect |
|---|---|---|
| Inventory | Fabricated purchases, inflated in-transit/on-hand quantities | Counts can be masked by "messy warehouse management" |
| **<mark>Goodwill</mark>** | High-premium acquisitions; pumping profit into targets to avoid impairment | Valuation assumptions are extremely subjective |
| Construction in progress | Fabricated project progress; funds parked as "unfinished" | Verification and audit are difficult |
| Cash | Restricted funds (pledged, guaranteed) disclosed as available cash | Signature of **<mark>high cash and high debt</mark>** |

> Fraud always leaves traces: **inflated revenue piles up in receivables, inflated assets pile up in inventory/CIP, hidden liabilities show up as mismatches between interest expense and cash flow.** The reconciliation gaps between statements are the fraudster's fingerprints.

::: warning ⚠️ Counterintuitive: Fraud Always Leaves Reconciliation Gaps
**Inflated revenue piles up in receivables, inflated assets pile up in inventory/CIP, hidden liabilities show up as interest-cash-flow mismatches.** A fraudster can fudge one statement but rarely reconciles three at once — reading reports closely means hunting these gaps; the wider the gap, the more water in the numbers.
:::

## 3. Case Review: Luckin Coffee (2020, Convicted Public Case)

::: info 📖 Note: The Luckin Case Is Publicly Settled Fact
Luckin's fraud was confirmed by US SEC proceedings and the company's own investigation, followed by settlement, delisting, and heavy fines — it is **publicly settled fact**, suitable as teaching material.
:::

### Timeline (Brief)

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| May 2019 | Luckin lists in the US on a story of "low prices + rapid expansion" |
| January 2020 | Short seller Muddy Waters publishes an 89-page report alleging accounting fraud |
| April 2020 | Company discloses that executives including the COO fabricated roughly RMB 2.2 billion of revenue (later confirmed); shares plunge ~80% in one day |
| June 2020 | Delisted; subsequent SEC settlement and fines; management and responsible parties held accountable |

### How the Short Seller Found It (The Methodology Is the Point)

1. **Field investigation**: hired 92 full-time and 1,418 part-time staff to stake out stores, recording foot traffic and sales against per-store disclosures — finding severe overstatement.
2. **Surveillance footage**: counting heads via store CCTV to cross-validate customer traffic.
3. **Related-party tracing**: uncovering suppliers (coffee machines, raw-material vendors) linked to executives/controllers, suspected of recycling funds through related-party procurement.
4. **Data contradictions**: disclosed per-store daily sales diverged hugely from field counts; app review data contradicted sales claims.
5. **Financial structure analysis**: hypergrowth revenue whose expense and cash-flow structures could not withstand scrutiny.

### Lessons for Ordinary Investors

- **The combination "growth story + burn-money expansion + constant fundraising" is not itself proof of fraud — but it earns a read of the financials down to the last line.**
- Numbers self-reported by listed companies must be cross-checked against third-party verifiable facts (stores, sales volumes, logistics, headcount).
- Short reports are not always right, but **their research methodology is learnable by every investor**: visit in person, count heads, trace related parties.

## 4. The "Three Highs" Signals: High Receivables, High Inventory, High Cash-Flow Divergence

| Signal | Concrete Signs | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| High receivables | Receivables growth persistently far above revenue growth; aging lengthening | Revenue may be "sold on credit", or never transacted at all |
| High inventory | Inventory surging continuously; turnover days rising year by year | Obsolescence risk, or costs parked in inventory |
| High cash-flow divergence | Net profit growing fast while operating cash flow stagnates or turns negative | Book profits without real money behind them |

The "three highs" are not proof of fraud — they are **the highest-priority health checks**. Two of the three warrant running the full 10-item checklist from [three-statements.md](three-statements.md).

::: info 📖 What the "Three Highs" Mean
**<mark>The three highs</mark>** (high receivables, high inventory, high cash-flow divergence) are **the highest-priority health checks** — two of them warrant a complete sweep with the 10-item earnings-quality checklist.

## 5. Audit Opinion Types: The Accountant's "Health Report"

Annual reports of listed companies must be audited by an accounting firm issuing an opinion — a free third-party check:

| Opinion Type | Meaning | Investor Response |
|---|---|---|
| **<mark>Unqualified opinion</mark>** (standard) | Statements fairly presented in all material respects | Pass normally |
| Unqualified + emphasis-of-matter paragraph | Fair statements, but the auditor flags something (e.g., going-concern doubt) | **Raise alertness**; read every emphasized item |
| Qualified opinion | Some items unverifiable or improperly treated, though not a wholesale rejection | **Major red flag**; usually key items have problems |
| Disclaimer of opinion | The auditor cannot even obtain a basis for judgment — "cannot say" | **Extremely dangerous**; effectively outside normal investing logic |
| Adverse opinion | Statements deemed materially misstated | Worse than a disclaimer; rare in A-shares |

::: warning ⚠️ Rule of Thumb: The Worse the Audit Opinion, the Higher the Risk
But note: an unqualified opinion does not mean the statements are true — audits are sample-based, and companies shop for accommodating firms (switching auditors is itself a signal). Subject to the latest accounting standards.
:::

## 6. The Detection Toolbox: 13 High-Frequency Red Flags

| # | Red Flag | Logic |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Abnormally stable gross margin | Industry and peers fluctuate; this one doesn't budge — numbers may be "set" |
| 2 | Persistently deteriorating receivables turnover | Losing bargaining power, or revenue inflation |
| 3 | Long-term divergence between cash flow and profit | Profit never converts into cash |
| 4 | Frequent share pledges by major shareholders | Controller needs money; heavy pressure to sell down or cash out |
| 5 | Serial acquisitions, elevated goodwill | Using M&A to manufacture growth and profit; impairment mine follows |
| 6 | Frequent auditor changes | Disagreements, or the firm refusing to cooperate |
| 7 | High cash and high debt | Large deposits + large high-rate borrowings; funds possibly diverted or circulating off-books |
| 8 | "Precision guidance-beating" | Hitting commitments/financing conditions exactly every year — coincidence too perfect to be true |
| 9 | Highly concentrated receivable customers | Revenue concentrated among a few "big clients" makes fabrication cheaper |
| 10 | Sudden upheaval among top-five suppliers/customers | Sign of a deliberately constructed relationship network |
| 11 | CIP never capitalized | Funds stuck in "half-finished" state, hard to verify |
| 12 | Frequent changes in accounting policies/estimates | Polishing numbers by changing measurement bases |
| 13 | Complex revenue recognition models | The harder recognition is to verify, the easier to manipulate |

::: warning ⚠️ One Red Flag Warrants Caution; Many Red Flags Signal Danger
A single red flag only raises vigilance — **danger is signaled only when multiple <mark>red flags</mark> appear together**. Conversely, few red flags don't mean absolute safety, just lower probability.
:::

## 7. How to Query Regulatory Enforcement Databases

### CSRC (China)

- The official site's "CSRC News" and "Administrative Penalties" sections publish penalty decisions with **specific violations, amounts, and responsible persons**.
- Penalty cases can be reverse-checked: did penalized companies show any red flags from this section earlier?
- Exchange websites (SSE/SZSE/BSE) disclose violators under "Disciplinary Actions" and "Regulatory Measures".

### Exchange Inquiry Letters — "An Inquiry Letter Is a Free Analyst"

| Channel | Content | Usage |
|---|---|---|
| Annual-report inquiry letters | Exchanges question anomalous annual-report items one by one | **Read word by word**: the questions asked are the exam points regulators marked for you |
| Restructuring inquiry letters | Questions on target valuations and earnings commitments | Assess commitment plausibility |
| Concern/supervision letters | Immediate follow-ups on unusual share moves or major events | Judge the quality of replies |

- Where to look: SSE/SZSE websites under "Information Disclosure → Regulatory Information → Inquiry Letters"; financial data platforms' dedicated inquiry-letter sections.
- **How to use**: company receives an **<mark>inquiry letter</mark>** on its annual report → whatever the exchange asks, investigate exactly that → check whether the reply has substance and internally consistent data.
- Classic scenario: the exchange asks "please explain whether the top five receivable customers have related-party relationships with the company" — that single sentence alone justifies blacklisting the company or digging deep.

::: danger 💀 Iron Law: Exchange Inquiry Letters Are Free Analysts
**An inquiry letter is a free analyst.** Whatever the exchange asks, dig there — the points it marks are often precisely the minefields retail investors find hardest to spot themselves. When the exchange presses on "related-party ties among the top five receivable customers", the answer usually justifies blacklisting the company or digging deep.
:::
