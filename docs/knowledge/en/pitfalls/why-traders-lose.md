# Why Traders Lose

> Retail traders never lose because of "bad luck". The vast majority of losses can be attributed to a handful of highly repetitive behavioral patterns — they do not care about your education, your account size, or whether the market is bullish or bearish; they just keep draining your account dry, slowly.

::: warning ⚠️ Risk Warning
This article is a behavioral-level summary intended for learning and research only; it does not constitute investment advice. All cases are objective descriptions of typical scenarios. **Every trade carries the risk of loss; participate only with money you can afford to lose.**
:::


---

## Before We Start: The Truth About Losses

One often-overlooked fact: **the share of traders who can profit steadily over the long run is extremely small; the vast majority of participants eventually leave at a loss.** The reason is not that "the market is a dark place" but that the nine behavioral patterns below keep harvesting retail traders over and over.

| The nine major causes of losses | In one sentence |
|---|---|
| ① Trading without a plan | Enter and exit on gut feeling; when you lose, you don't even know why |
| ② Excessive **<mark>leverage</mark>** | Using 100x leverage turns you into a lottery ticket |
| ③ Holding losers with no **<mark>stop-loss</mark>** | Turning a "temporary loss" into a permanent **<mark>zero</mark>** |
| ④ Overtrading and fee attrition | Worn down by fees before the market ever beats you |
| ⑤ Chasing highs and panic-selling lows (FOMO) | Buying at the top, capitulating at the bottom — perfectly wrong-footed |
| ⑥ Catching falling knives | "The more it falls, the more I buy" — inside a downtrend |
| ⑦ Averaging down on losers | Covering one mistake with a bigger mistake |
| ⑧ Overconfidence and oversized single-stock positions | One right call buries your entire principal |
| ⑨ Trusting insider tips and influencer picks | Handing your decision power to a "stranger" |

**Remember this line first: a <mark>stop-loss</mark> is a trader's professional discipline, while "riding a loser" is a retail trader's grave.** Now let's dissect them one by one.

## ① Trading Without a Plan

### What it looks like

No pre-market plan, no **<mark>position</mark>** budget, no take-profit or **<mark>stop-loss</mark>** — open the app and place orders on "feel". When you win, you don't know why; when you lose, you don't know why either — and when luck hands you a profit, you mistake it for skill.

### A realistic scenario

Zhang sees a bullish news item and immediately goes all-in on a coin. Price rises first, then falls; he never decided "where to **<mark>take profit</mark>**, where to stop out", can't bring himself to sell the unrealized gain, and after the profit evaporates the next day he tells himself "I'll sell at breakeven" — then watches it fall all the way to a 50% drawdown and finally capitulates at the low. Looking back: **not a single step of this trade was repeatable or verifiable.**

### Psychological roots

- **Impulse for instant gratification**: the thrill of the click is more immediate than rational thought.
- **Outcome-oriented, not process-oriented**: only asking "did this trade make money", never "was this trade done right".
- **Avoiding planning**: writing a plan means admitting uncertainty; winging it lets you blame "bad market conditions".

### The antidote

- **Take five minutes to write three lines before any order**: why buy, where to stop out, how big a position. If you cannot write them, you are not allowed to place the order.
- Log the entry/exit rationale for every trade and review weekly, tracking "luck trades" and "plan trades" separately — you will find the **<mark>win rate</mark>** of plan trades is far higher than that of luck trades.
- Strictly follow the three steps "plan first, execute second, review last" — none can be skipped.

## ② Excessive Leverage

### What it looks like

Treating leverage as a money printer: 10x not enough, use 50x; 50x not enough, use 100x. For the **<mark>leverage</mark>** basics (at 10x, a 10% drop means zero), see [Trading Core Concepts](../getting-started/core-concepts.md）.

### A realistic scenario

Li opens a 100x long in a crypto perpetual. The market pulls back just 1% and his position is <mark>liquidated</mark> — principal gone. He sighs, "it was just normal fluctuation" — exactly: **at 100x leverage, a 1% adverse move is enough to knock you out**, and a 1% move happens in crypto almost every day.

### Psychological roots

- **Greed and the get-rich-quick illusion**: wanting maximum returns from minimum capital.
- **Gambler's mentality**: after losing, reach for higher leverage to "win it back"; after winning, dream of "retiring on one trade".
- **No feel for probability**: only computing "how much if I'm right", never "how much if I'm wrong — and how many wrongs until I'm out".

### The antidote

- Beginners should cap leverage at **3x or lower**, and derivatives traders should keep per-trade risk within **1%~2%** of total capital.
- Before every entry, do the math: **at what % adverse move do I get <mark>liquidated</mark>? How often has a move of that size occurred historically?**
- Write the liquidation price into your trading plan — the higher the leverage, the tighter the stop must be, because your margin for error shrinks.

## ③ Holding Losers Without a Stop-Loss

### What it looks like

Refusing to admit defeat when losing, believing "it will always come back", and holding all the way. A small loss becomes a big loss; a big loss ends in liquidation or being stuck underwater. "It isn't a loss until I sell" is the retail trader's greatest self-consolation — and the most expensive tuition.

### A realistic scenario

Wang buys a stock at 8; it drops to 6 and he comforts himself with "value investing"; at 4 he starts to panic; at 3 he capitulates and exits. The price later bounces back to 7 — **he was present for the entire decline and perfectly missed the rise.** Even more common is the derivatives version: while riding the loser he keeps adding <mark>margin</mark>, until everything is liquidated in one shot at the **<mark>forced-liquidation price</mark>**.

### Psychological roots

- **Loss aversion**: cutting the loss means the pain of "admitting a mistake", which is harder to accept than an unrealized drawdown.
- **Anchoring**: the mind stays anchored to the **<mark>entry price</mark>**, replacing objective judgment with "getting back to breakeven".
- **Sunk-cost fallacy**: the deeper the loss, the more "I've held this long — leaving now would cost even more".

### The antidote

- **Place the stop-loss order at the same moment you open the position** — it must be part of the order workflow, not something to set "when I get around to it".
- Derive the stop distance from "max risk per trade = 1%~2% of total capital", not from a number pulled out of thin air.
- Keep reminding yourself: **the market does not know your cost basis.** Your entry price is your own psychological anchor, not something the market owes you.

## ④ Overtrading and Fee Attrition

### What it looks like

Opening and closing positions several times a day, enjoying every minute of it, while never accounting for a hidden gold-eating monster — **fees and <mark>slippage</mark>**. The high-frequency trader's real opponent is often not the market but the exchange's fee schedule.

### A realistic scenario (do the math)

Assume 10 trades a day (one round trip counts as one), with a 0.1% fee per side (on many platforms the effective cost stays above this even after rebates). Ten round trips a day = 20 one-sided charges:

- Daily fees = 20 × 0.1% = **2% of principal**
- Over 250 trading days a year = 250 × 2% = **500% of principal**

**Conclusion: even if every trade breaks even, fees will eat the equivalent of 5x your principal in a year.** That means your annualized **<mark>return</mark>** must exceed 500% just to break even — this is not trading, it is working for the exchange.

| Trades per day | At 0.1% per side | Annual fee drag (250 days) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 0.2%/day | 50% of principal |
| 5 | 1%/day | 250% of principal |
| 10 | 2%/day | 500% of principal |
| 20 | 4%/day | 1000% of principal |

### Psychological roots

- **Hyperactive anxiety**: being out of a position feels unbearable — "if I don't act, I'm missing out".
- **Mistaking participation for profit**: placing the order is itself a source of pleasure.
- **Ignoring hidden costs**: fees are not as eye-catching as losses, so they get systematically overlooked.

### The antidote

- **Count fees into every trade's cost**: before entering, compute "how much does this have to rise just to cover fees + <mark>slippage</mark>".
- Set a quota of "at most N trades per week" to force yourself to take only high-quality setups.
- Accept one fact: **an empty position is also a position, and waiting is also an action.**

## ⑤ Chasing Highs and Panic-Selling Lows (FOMO)

### What it looks like

Rushing in on a big rally for fear of missing out (FOMO), and cutting losses in a panic on a big drop for fear of worse. The behavior lands perfectly on the wrong side of market sentiment: **retail traders take over the handoffs at the highs, capitulate at the lows, and repeat the cycle countless times.**

### A realistic scenario

A coin doubles in a week; the group chat is full of "get on board before it's too late". Chen goes all-in at the tail end of the rally; the market then pulls back 30% and he dumps everything the moment he hears "the bear market is here". A month later the price returns to his cost — **he not only missed the entire move but also booked a very real 30% loss.** Chasing and panic-selling usually occur within the same person's same round of trading.

### Psychological roots

- **FOMO (fear of missing out)**: watching others make money hurts more than losing money yourself.
- **Herd effect**: group emotion overrides independent thinking.
- **Emotion hijacking reason**: greed and fear are strong emotions, and emotional orders never follow logic.

### The antidote

- Institute a "FOMO cooling-off period": when you see a big move, wait 24 hours first and ask yourself, "if I held no position right now, would I buy at this price?"
- Enter only at trigger points defined in your plan — **a rally outside your plan is none of your business, however high it climbs.**
- Remember: the market always has a next bus — **what you missed is not "the last chance ever", only "this one chance".**

## ⑥ Catching Falling Knives

### What it looks like

"It has fallen so much, this must be the bottom" — so the lower it falls, the more they buy, until they discover there is a basement below the basement. **A trend reversal is not judged by "cheapness", and the biggest retail mistake is "treating a decline as a discount".**

### A realistic scenario

A coin falls from 100 to 60; Zhou thinks "40% cheaper" and buys. It falls to 40 and he adds "to lower the average". At 25 he panics, right? No — he keeps buying, until it hits 10, his capital runs out, and he sits on a 90% unrealized loss. Throughout the whole process, **not a single objective signal confirmed the decline had ended** — all he ever bought was "the feeling of cheap".

### Psychological roots

- **Anchoring to the old price**: using the historical high as the reference point, feeling "the farther from the top, the safer".
- **Gambler's fallacy**: "it has fallen so much, the next move should be up" — but random sequences have no memory.
- **Self-persuasion**: to rationalize the bottom-fishing, actively hunting for bullish news and selectively ignoring risk.

### The antidote

- **Never catch a falling knife; only buy "confirmed landings"**: wait for price to stabilize and a clear bottom structure to appear (e.g., a high-volume long lower wick, a trendline break) before considering entry.
- Restrict "bottom-fishing" to **staged, small, DCA-style buys**, with a hard capital cap for each lower tier — never go all-in at once.
- Ask yourself one question: **if this asset fell another 50%, could I still hold it?** If not, don't fish.

## ⑦ Averaging Down on Losses

### What it looks like

After a loss, keep pouring in more money to "lower the average cost". This occasionally works in bull-market pullbacks, so it gets treated as a universal strategy — until a genuine trending decline arrives: **the more you average, the more you lose; the more you lose, the more you average, until both ammunition and capital are exhausted.**

### A realistic scenario

Wu buys at 10; it drops to 8 and he doubles up to "average down", bringing his cost to 9; it drops to 6 and he adds again, cost now 7.5. With his capital exhausted, the stock keeps falling to 3. **He lost 3x his original principal in exchange for the psychological comfort of "a lower cost price" — a cost price that means nothing whatsoever to the market.** The futures version is worse: averaging down is essentially "riding a loser + amplifying leverage" combined — one adverse move can trigger **<mark>forced liquidation</mark>** of every layer at once.

### Psychological roots

- **Revenge trading (the gambler's "double or nothing")**: using a bigger stake to bury the fact of a loss.
- **Cost-basis obsession**: treating "lowering the average" as the goal itself, forgetting the goal is "to make money".
- **Sunk cost**: money already lost makes it hard to let go.

### The antidote

- **Clearly separate "planned add-ons" from "loss averaging"**: the former is based on stabilization signals and comes with position caps; the latter is based on the emotion of "wanting to break even" — the latter is banned outright.
- Set an iron rule for yourself: **once an unrealized loss on a position exceeds 10%, no adding of any kind is allowed.**
- Remember the math: recovering a 50% loss requires a 100% gain; recovering an 80% loss requires a 400% gain. **Using add-ons to "rescue" a losing trade is one of the most expensive strategies in trading.**

## ⑧ Overconfidence and Oversized Single-Stock Positions

### What it looks like

After one or two winning trades, decide you have "found the Holy Grail" and go heavy — even all-in — on a single asset. **Even a trader with a 60% win rate will sooner or later <mark>zero out</mark> if they bet the whole book every time — position sizing matters no less than direction.**

### A realistic scenario

Liu calls three spot moves correctly in a row and his confidence explodes. He concentrates all his savings plus borrowed money into one "potential doubler" and tells his family "this one is certain". The stock then hits consecutive limit-downs on an earnings blowup — **he not only lost the principal but is now saddled with a loan he must repay month after month.** Three small wins were wiped out by one zero-tolerance heavy position.

### Psychological roots

- **Overconfidence bias**: success gets over-generalized into "I'm smarter than the market".
- **Confirmation bias**: collecting only information that supports the call, ignoring opposing signals.
- **The all-in rush**: the adrenaline of betting everything pushes reason aside.

### The antidote

- **Hard cap per position**: a single stock/instrument no more than 10%~20% of total capital (≤10% recommended for beginners).
- **Always leave a way out for "I'm wrong"**: assume any given trade might be "100% wrong" and size it accordingly.
- Use "if this position went to zero, would my life be affected?" as the bottom-line test — **a position that would affect your life is a position that is too big.**

## ⑨ Trusting Insider Tips and Influencer Picks

### What it looks like

Abandon independent judgment and hand decision power to "insider information", "gurus", or "influencers". Set aside whether the tip is even true — **even if it is genuine, by the time it reaches you it is already priced in; and more often, the "tip" you receive is itself somebody else's exit liquidity.**

### A realistic scenario

Zhao joins a "market analyst" group chat. The group owner posts profit screenshots for three straight days (later revealed to be from a demo account). On day four, the owner's "insider tip" recommends a coin; Zhao goes heavy, the price spikes and instantly waterfalls, and the owner blocks everyone. The news later exposes it as a pig-butchering scam: **the so-called "guru" sold into the highs while retail traders became the bagholders.** (For a full teardown of this type of scam, see [02-Scam Detection](scam-detection.md).)

### Psychological roots

- **Effort aversion**: letting someone else decide is far easier than doing your own research.
- **Authority worship**: titles like "influencer" or "analyst" come with a built-in halo.
- **Loss aversion and blame-shifting**: if you lose following someone else's advice, you can blame them and never have to face "my decision was wrong".

### The antidote

- **Iron rule: any stock/coin tip can only serve as a research lead, never as an order trigger.**
- Think in reverse about "insider tips": **if this were truly a can't-lose secret, why would they tell me instead of quietly buying it themselves?**
- The bottom line of independent decisions: **you may place the order only if you can explain the trade's logic in your own words.**

## Trader Self-Diagnostic Checklist

The 10 items below each correspond to a wound that is actively bleeding. **Re-check yourself against them once a month; if 3 or more hit, stop trading and review.**

| # | Diagnostic item | Related cause |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Do I often place orders without writing a trading plan? | ① No plan |
| 2 | Is my leverage above 3x (as a beginner), or can't I blurt out my own liquidation price? | ② Leverage |
| 3 | When underwater, have I thought "just wait a bit, it will come back" instead of setting a stop? | ③ Riding losers |
| 4 | Did I trade more than 10 times last week? Do I ever count fees? | ④ Overtrading |
| 5 | Have I chased rallies out of "fear of missing out", or panic-sold out of fear? | ⑤ FOMO |
| 6 | Have I bought during a decline, comforting myself that "it's already cheap"? | ⑥ Falling knives |
| 7 | Have I ever added to a losing position to "lower the average cost"? | ⑦ Averaging down |
| 8 | Have I ever let a single trade/single stock exceed 20% of total capital? | ⑧ Oversized positions |
| 9 | Have I ever placed an order directly off a message from some influencer or group chat? | ⑨ Tips |
| 10 | Can I review last month's trades and articulate "what was right, what was wrong"? | All |

### Interpreting your score

- **0~2 items**: healthy state; keep up the planning discipline.
- **3~5 items**: danger zone — **pause live trading for 2 weeks**, trade demo only, and rebuild your process.
- **6+ items**: you are being "eliminated" by yourself. Stop trading, reread every "antidote" in this article, or seek professional help.

### One final piece of advice

::: danger 💀 Your emotions are doing the trading for you
**Behind all nine causes there is only one common theme: your emotions are doing the trading for you.** Skills can be learned and systems can be built, but if you do not deal with emotion and discipline, every dollar you make is only parked in your account for a while — sooner or later the market takes it back with interest.
:::
