# 04 · Licensing and Market Access

> "Is this platform trustworthy?" "Is this firm a real, licensed player?" — the answer isn't in the advertising; it's in the licenses. Financial licenses are the moat of financial companies and the only hard yardstick traders have for telling "who is legit". This article explains China's financial license landscape, broker/futures/fund sub-qualifications, and overseas licensing systems (Hong Kong Type 1–13, US FINRA/SEC), then teaches you to verify any institution on official websites in three minutes.

::: warning ⚠️ Risk Warning
This article is an objective compilation of public knowledge, for study and research only, and **does not constitute legal advice**. License categories, application requirements, and regulatory rules change continuously — **all specifics are subject to the latest regulations**. Holding a license does not mean no risk; judge holistically based on platform history and product risk.
:::

## 1. China's Financial License Landscape

### 1.1 Major Licenses at a Glance

| License | Issuer | Business | Relative Barrier |
|---|---|---|---|
| Banking license | NFRA | Taking deposits, issuing loans | Extremely high |
| Securities license | CSRC | Securities brokerage, underwriting, asset management, proprietary trading | High |
| Fund license (public) | CSRC | Public fund raising and management | High |
| Fund registration (private) | AMAC filing system | Private fund managers | Medium (filing-based) |
| Futures license | CSRC | Futures brokerage, consulting, asset management | High |
| Insurance license | NFRA | Life / property insurance | Extremely high |
| Payment license | PBOC | Payment institutions (non-bank payments) | High |
| Micro-lending license | Local financial regulators | Small-scale lending | Medium-low |
| Financing guarantee / leasing | Local financial regulators | Finance-like businesses | Medium-low |

**Common sense**: a license's essence is "**<mark>franchise rights</mark> + conduct constraints**" — you can't just open one, and once open you must operate by regulatory rules.

![Map of China's financial licenses: a three-layer structure of regulators, licensed institutions, and the businesses facing you](_assets/license-map.svg)

::: tip 💡 Official website checks are always free and authoritative
**Any "legitimacy" can be packaged, but official website verification is always free and authoritative.** Spending three minutes verifying the license number, the receiving account, and penalty records beats spending a night on complaint hotlines after losing money.
:::

### 1.2 Why "A License Is a Financial Company's Moat"

| Dimension | Explanation |
|---|---|
| Scarcity | Approval-based supply keeps licenses limited, especially banking, securities, insurance |
| Capital barriers | Registered capital often runs from hundreds of millions to billions of yuan; newcomers struggle to enter |
| License premium | A securities brokerage license can be worth far more than the company's book net assets (the so-called "shell price") |
| Conduct constraints | Licensed firms must stay continuously compliant; violations bring downgrades, business suspensions, even <mark>revocation</mark> |
| For users | License = funds have a regulatory backstop (bank tri-party custody, client **<mark>margin</mark>** monitoring) |

::: warning ⚠️ Reverse common sense: a license doesn't directly protect users
The moat protects the licensee, **not users directly** — a license guarantees "legitimate operation", never "no losses". You check licenses to avoid scammers, not to trust blindly.
:::

## 2. Brokerage License Sub-Qualifications

### 2.1 Four Core Business Qualifications of Securities Firms

| Qualification | Content | Typical Clients |
|---|---|---|
| Securities brokerage | Buying/selling stocks/funds/bonds for clients, charging commissions | Retail and institutional |
| Underwriting & sponsorship (investment banking) | Helping companies IPO, refinance, merge; heavy sponsor liability | Listed companies |
| Proprietary trading | The broker investing its own money | The broker itself |
| Asset management | Discretionary wealth management (asset management plans/public fund subsidiaries) | High-net-worth and institutional |

### 2.2 Broker Classification and Regulation

- The CSRC rates brokers by **risk management capability and compliance record** (AA, A, BBB…C); ratings affect business scope and contributions to the investor protection fund.
- Investment banking projects carry **joint liability**: sponsors bear joint responsibility for disclosure fraud (significantly strengthened under registration reform).
- Brokers must also meet **net capital and risk control indicator** requirements — regulators use capital constraints to limit risk expansion.

**What this means for retail investors**: choosing a **highly rated, listed, long-established top broker** is essentially choosing a counterparty with thicker capital and risk control; but trading experience and commission rates matter too — high rating does not mean low fees.

## 3. Futures Company Licenses and Classified Supervision

### 3.1 Futures Business Scope

| Business | Notes |
|---|---|
| Commodity futures brokerage | Basic business; nearly all futures companies have it |
| Financial futures brokerage | Requires separate approval |
| Futures investment consulting | Strategy advice (distinguish from "signal calling": licensed consulting ≠ pump groups) |
| Asset management | Futures asset management plans |
| Risk management subsidiaries | OTC options, **<mark>basis</mark>** trading, warehouse receipt services (institutional-facing) |

### 3.2 Classified Supervision

- The CSRC/China Futures Association rate futures companies in classes AA through D; higher classes unlock more innovation businesses.
- Futures companies are also subject to **net capital regulation**: margin scale is tied to their net capital, capping each firm's total risk capacity.
- Client margins are held under **independent custody** (monitored centrally by the China Futures Market Monitoring Center) — client funds are not in the futures company's own accounts; this is the industry's most fundamental safety design.

## 4. Public vs Private Fund Licensing

### 4.1 Public Funds

| Dimension | Content |
|---|---|
| Regulator | CSRC approval (public fund license); raises from the **general public** |
| Barriers | Rigorous review of shareholder strength, registered capital, investment teams, risk systems |
| Products | Equity/bond/money market/balanced/ETF/index funds |
| Distribution | Sold to the mass market via banks, brokers, internet platforms |
| Character | Strong disclosure (daily NAV, quarterly/annual reports), tight operation rules (investment scope and ratio limits) |

### 4.2 Private Funds (Filing-Based)

| Dimension | Common-Sense Points |
|---|---|
| Regulator | **AMAC registration and filing system**: manager registration + product filing, not approval-based |
| Registered capital | Registration requires **paid-in capital above a threshold** (historically RMB 10 million; see current AMAC registration guidelines) |
| Personnel | Must hold **sufficient certified staff** (fund practitioner qualifications) plus compliance/risk officers |
| <mark>Qualified investors</mark> | Minimum investment typically **RMB 1 million** per product; personal financial assets ≥ RMB 3 million or average annual income ≥ RMB 500,000 over 3 years |
| Sales restrictions | No public solicitation; only qualified investors may be approached |

::: warning ⚠️ Common-sense reminder: filing is not regulatory endorsement

- **Private ≠ an "upgraded" public fund**: private funds carry high thresholds because risk is high, not because returns are high.
- "Filed" does not mean "endorsed": AMAC filing proves formal compliance only — **<mark>product risk and losses receive no protection whatsoever because of a filing</mark>**. Many collapsed private funds had filing numbers (see the collapse playbook in [Chapter 08 · Pitfalls](../pitfalls/)).
- First step when encountering a "private product": check the manager's registration status, abnormal-operation records, and related-party penalties on the AMAC website.
:::

## 5. Overseas Licenses

### 5.1 Hong Kong SFC Regulated Activity Licenses in Detail

| Type | Regulated Activity | Plain-Language Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Dealing in securities | Stock brokerage |
| 2 | Dealing in futures contracts | Futures brokerage |
| 3 | **<mark>Leveraged</mark>** foreign exchange trading | FX margin trading |
| 4 | Advising on securities | Stock advisory |
| 5 | Advising on futures contracts | Futures advisory |
| 6 | Advising on corporate finance | Investment banking/sponsorship |
| 7 | Providing automated trading services | Electronic trading platforms |
| 8 | Securities margin financing | Margin (financing) lending |
| 9 | Asset management | Discretionary wealth management |
| 10 | Providing credit rating services | Credit rating |
| 11 | OTC derivatives trading | OTC derivatives |
| 12 | OTC derivatives clearing | OTC clearing |
| 13 | Alternative asset funds (proposed) | Alternative assets |

> Note: Hong Kong currently recognizes **Types 1–12** as regulated activities; Type 13 ("alternative asset funds") is proposed — implementation timing is subject to the latest SFC announcements.

**Verification**: the SFC's Public Register shows licensed corporations' and individuals' license status, categories, and disciplinary records — a must-check before opening an HK/US brokerage account.

### 5.2 US FINRA Exam Basics

| Exam | Plain-Language Meaning | Common Among |
|---|---|---|
| Series 7 (General Securities Representative) | Core brokerage qualification: may sell stocks/funds/options etc. | Broker representatives |
| Series 63 (State Securities Law) | State securities law compliance knowledge | Broker representatives (required by most states) |
| Series 65 | Investment adviser exam (RIA personnel) | Investment advisers |
| Series 66 | Combined 63+65 | Broker-representative advisers |
| Series 3 | National Commodity Futures exam (NFA-related) | Futures brokerage |
| Series 24 | General securities principal | Broker management roles |

**Common sense**: FINRA registrants can be looked up at **FINRA BrokerCheck (brokercheck.finra.org)** — disciplinary history, complaints, penalties. It's the public channel for checking whether your US stock broker is reliable (also proactively check a broker's SEC/FINRA registration status when opening an account).

### 5.3 SEC RIA (Registered Investment Adviser)

| Dimension | Content |
|---|---|
| Concept | Registered Investment Adviser: firms giving advice under a "<mark>fiduciary duty</mark>" (private client asset management, advisory) |
| Registration | Firms above a certain AUM threshold (on the order of USD 100 million; per SEC rules) register with the SEC; smaller ones with states |
| Fees | Typically charge management fees on AUM rather than trading commissions |
| vs broker-dealers | Brokers "execute trades" (Reg BI best interest); RIAs "manage assets" (fiduciary duty) — stricter conflict-of-interest disclosure |

## 6. "Buying a Shell vs Applying for a License": The Reality

### 6.1 Comparing Two Paths

| Path | Cost | Time | Risks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Applying directly | Heavy capital + compliance investment | Long (months to years) | Approval uncertainty |
| Acquiring a licensed company (shell purchase) | Hundreds of millions to billions (securities/futures) | Fast | Shell's historical compliance issues, liabilities, legacy penalties |

**Common sense**: buying a shell is essentially purchasing the "scarce administrative permit" itself — regulators also approve ownership changes, so **buying a shell doesn't automatically transfer shareholders**. History offers plenty of cases of "buy shell, then blow up", because shell buyers often arrive with leverage and aggressive business ambitions.

### 6.2 Common Disguises of Unlicensed Operation

| Disguise | Truth |
|---|---|
| "We run on some licensed broker's white label" | May merely rent a channel; fund custody and compliance responsibility unclear |
| "Overseas license, domestic operations" | Domestic <mark>unlicensed operation</mark> violates regulations outright (see [crypto-regulation.md](crypto-regulation.md)) |
| "Application in progress" | Applying ≠ licensed; regulated business before licensing is prohibited |
| Stolen/counterfeited license numbers | Instantly exposed by checking official websites |

## 7. What It Means for Traders: Identifying Licensed Institutions in Three Minutes

### 7.1 Official Verification Channels

| What I Want to Verify | Where to Check |
|---|---|
| Is a broker/futures company/fund manager licensed | CSRC website "Regulatory Information Disclosure" → list of securities and futures institutions |
| Is a private fund manager registered | AMAC website (amac.org.cn) |
| Banks/insurers/payment institutions | NFRA website, PBOC website |
| Hong Kong licensed institutions | SFC Public Register |
| US brokers/advisers | FINRA BrokerCheck, SEC website |
| US futures brokers | NFA BASIC database |

### 7.2 Five-Step Verification Method

1. **Match the license number**: the institution's full name and license number on official records must match exactly what they claim.
2. **Match the entity**: the receiving account must belong to the licensed institution itself — **never personal accounts or third-party collectors**.
3. **Check records**: look for administrative penalties, disciplinary actions, abnormal operations (especially for private funds).
4. **Check the domain**: official domain must match the registered entity; fake sites often differ by one letter.
5. **Reverse verification**: search suspicious platforms by name on regulator websites — no hit or mismatch means automatic veto.

::: tip 💡 Final common sense: three minutes of verification beats after-the-fact recourse
Any "legitimacy" can be packaged, but **official website verification is always free and authoritative**. Three minutes of checking beats spending all night on complaint calls after losing money.
:::
